## The end of the anonymous era of reporting

Until 2026, the IRS effectively had no standardized way to learn from exchanges themselves what a given crypto holder sold and at what price — reporting relied on taxpayers' good faith. Form 1099-DA changes that: centralized exchanges and other custodial brokers must file it with the IRS and send a copy to the user, recording the fact and amount of sales of digital assets through that venue over the year.

## Why the first wave of forms is incomplete

For 2025, exchanges reported only gross sale proceeds — without [cost basis](/en/glossary/#cost-basis), because the infrastructure for tracking cost basis for each specific coin wasn't yet ready by the deadline. A full calculation of profit or loss based on this form alone was impossible: taxpayers still had to reconstruct their cost basis themselves.

## What changes with 2026 trades

Starting with trades made in tax year 2026 (forms arrive in early 2027), exchanges must report cost basis — but only for "covered" assets: those bought on that same exchange after January 1, 2026 and kept in its custodial account until sale. If a coin was bought before 2026 or moved between different wallets and exchanges before sale, the asset is "noncovered": the form will show only the sale amount, marked as such, and calculating cost basis and final gain remains up to the user.

## Who this affects in practice

The difference between a "covered" and a "noncovered" asset means, in practice, that almost anyone who has traded crypto for over a year, or even once moved coins between a wallet and an exchange, will fall into a category where the exchange won't work out everything for them. Combined with the IRS's 2026 broadening of the very notion of a "digital asset" (which now explicitly includes NFTs, stablecoins, and many DeFi instruments) and the CARF rules obliging platforms to collect user data for future cross-border information exchange from 2027, this is one of the few regulatory changes of 2026 that directly and immediately changes what the tax authority sees about a specific person — not just the rules of the game for exchanges and issuers.
